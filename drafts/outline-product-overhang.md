# Post 4 — The product overhang: outline

Working titles: *The Product Overhang* · *We Outran the Feedback* ·
*The Rate of Change Humans Will Accept* · *A Tale of Two Speeds*

**The one-line argument:** slow execution was accidentally providing the learning
cadence. We removed the slowness, the loop broke, and the binding constraint
turned out to be how fast humans will accept change — which is lower than anyone
priced in.

## 1. The observation

- For all the efficiency in engineering, shockingly little has reached me as a
  customer
- Products I use daily: not faster, not less buggy, not richer. Some haven't moved
- Keep the hedge — maybe they have and I'm not noticing
- **Since ChatGPT launched, how slow it's been in parts is the genuinely
  surprising thing.** Some tools take off, change fast. Elsewhere you log in and
  it's identical
- Lots of places have adopted no AI at all. *"Huh, okay."*

## 2. The obvious explanation — and why it's incomplete

- First answer: the efficiency is going into low-impact work. The backlog that
  never cleared the bar, the tidy-ups, the long tail
- We spent years saying *if only we had the capacity*. Now there's capacity and
  teams are working down a list of things that lost, repeatedly, on purpose
- **Concede this is partly true** — then say it isn't the main thing
- (This is post 1's ending. Compress it to a few lines here and move on — the
  real answer is better)

## 3. The loop we didn't know we were relying on

**This is the idea the post exists for.**

- The old rhythm on a roadmap — 6, 9 months of work, and everyone confident in it
- You'd ship a v1. Then fixes, improvements. Feedback starts arriving
- You move on to problem 2 — and while you're solving it, **the feedback on
  problem 1 lands**
- That's the learning loop, and it was load-bearing
- **The point:** the pace of execution *forced* a pace at which you had time to
  learn and get signal — so you knew what to do next
- Nobody designed that. It was a **side effect of things being slow**
- We removed the slowness and lost the thing it was accidentally providing

## 4. What breaks when you blitz the roadmap

- Now teams tear through the whole thing. Months of roadmap, done
- And then: **"What's next?"**
- And it's genuinely hard to answer, because **nothing has had enough time in
  market to tell you anything**
- You've spent your entire backlog of *informed* decisions. What's left has no
  evidence behind it yet
- Link to post 3 here if you want it: this is *why* the decision load gets
  brutal. It isn't just more decisions, it's more decisions with less signal

## 5. A tale of two speeds

- The question underneath all of this: **is the market moving faster than
  execution now?**
- Genuinely unclear, and it cuts both ways — **it seems like a tale of two speeds**
- **Fast:** some things get adopted extraordinarily quickly, big changes, real
  behaviour shifts
- **Slow:** try selling into companies and there's enormous caution and fear —
  - what will have to change
  - who needs training
  - people changing roles
  - security sign-off
  - and on
- So it is **not** "build a great product and boom"
- **You will not get usage and feedback as fast as you built the thing.** That
  sentence is the hinge of the post

## 6. The bottleneck moves

- The constraint used to be: can we build it
- Now it's: **can we get enough real usage, and good enough feedback, to know
  where to go next**
- Note the word *real* — usage that tells you something, not a signup number
- Building faster doesn't help. It may actively hurt: more shipped, less known
  about any of it

## 7. The overhang

- We talk about a **technology overhang** — capability sitting unused
- What we have now is a **product overhang** as well. The products exist. People
  aren't ready
- **The ceiling nobody costed: the rate of change humans will accept** — or the
  rate they'll move at
- And it turns out that number is **lower than we assumed**
- Ending: you can compress the building all you like. The part where a person
  changes how they work doesn't compress

## Why this is the strongest of the four

The other three are arguments about a job. This one is an argument about **where
the constraint actually sits**, and it's the least-said thing in your material.

"Product overhang" is your coinage and it's good. The line under it —
*the bottleneck is the rate of change humans will accept* — is the single most
quotable sentence across everything you've dictated.
