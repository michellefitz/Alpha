# When Did You Last Disagree With AI?

Everyone's written about AI being sycophantic. It agrees with you, it mirrors your framing back, it tells you your idea is great. [Anthropic's own research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548) traced it back to us rather than the machine — these models get tuned on human feedback, and what we rate highly is agreement. Nobody opens a chat hoping to hear their idea is mediocre.

The bit I don't hear talked about as much is what happens next. I've stopped checking.

Part of it is that it got really good. Hallucinations used to be the thing — the warning in every article, the reason you went and verified something before you used it. I honestly can't remember the last one I noticed, and I don't think about it at all anymore. Three or four years ago this was ask-a-question-get-an-answer, with a short memory and some fairly obvious mistakes. Now it does multi-step work and half the time goes further than I asked it to. That all crept in gradually, and my scepticism went with it.

The other part is softer, and it's the one I keep coming back to. Ask about a holiday and you get "Barcelona's lovely at that time of year." Ask about a light for the hallway and it tells you that's a good spot for one. Tiny things, but they're nice to hear. Someone agreed with me. And I'm way more likely to go along with something that's just gone along with me — it feels like it understood the question, so the answer's probably good too.

There are really two different ways I ask for things.

Sometimes I know what I want. I've got an eval in my head, basically — a reasonable idea of what a good answer looks like — and my job is to describe it well enough. If what comes back is wrong I'll spot it, because I had something to check it against.

The rest of the time I'm just asking. Recommend me something. Find me a light like this one. Plan this trip. What are the best ten. I've got no idea what the right answer looks like. That's the whole reason I'm asking.

So I've stopped challenging it. I push back when I already know the subject, or when it comes back with something obviously rubbish. The rest of the time it's: yeah, fine, that sounds good, let's start there. My husband sees me doing something the long way round and goes, why don't you just ask the AI? And he's usually right.

Which means I'm most sceptical about the things I already know, and least sceptical about everything else. Completely backwards.

## Everything is built to help you agree

The other thing I keep noticing is how much of the design pushes the same way.

There's suggested text everywhere now, sitting there waiting for you to finish it. My email replies get written for me and I press tab. It gives me the answer and then tells me what to do next, so I press tab again. I get three options and one of them says "recommended" — and yeah, okay, the recommended one sounds good to me, let's go with that, it's recommended.

In a chat it'll write your next question for you. Do you want me to do X next? And you say yes. Sometimes there's little helper text telling you that you can tap to complete. It's mad.

None of it's sinister on its own. But it all makes saying yes the easiest thing to do, and it keeps you moving down a path you didn't really pick.

That's the loop. It agrees with you, so you trust it. You trust it, so you take the recommendation. Then it offers you the next thing, and saying yes to that is one keystroke. Round you go again.

And the list of stuff I hand over keeps getting longer, because you don't know what it can do until you try.

We're redecorating part of the house at the moment. I'd written AI off for images — slow, always a bit off, close enough to be useless. Then I gave ChatGPT a photo and some architect's drawings, and it built 3D models of our rooms, mood boards, proper renders of each space. It's not perfect, some of the furniture sits at funny angles. But for the amount of effort it took me to ask, what came back was amazing.

So now I ask for images constantly. Last week I wanted to know which jacket went with an outfit, so I got it to generate pictures of me in each one.

That keeps happening. Something that didn't work a year ago quietly starts working, and the only way you find out is by going back and trying it again. You have to keep challenging what you assume it can't do. But the second it surprises you, it becomes a habit — one more thing you hand over and stop checking.

The thing I'm watching now is personal agents.

I've only just started using them, and it's very early — weeks, really. Mostly what's happening is people slowly realising they can ask for things at all. That's a new muscle to build: oh, I suppose I could get the agent to do that. I think it'll take most people ages.

Today I got mine to go through my inbox and unsubscribe from everything I haven't been opening. Someone had suggested it and I thought, oh yeah, I should do that. It worked, and straight away I was thinking, okay, what else can I get it to look at in there?

Same pattern again. And I don't want to overclaim here, because nobody really knows where this goes yet. But a recommendation that changes what you cancel, or buy, or book, is a different thing from a recommendation about your work. It's much closer to home, and it doesn't get weighed up. It just gets done.

And that's the bit I can't put down. We all assume these things are impartial. They're working from what they know, telling us what they found. Today that's probably mostly true. But a few hundred million people who've stopped questioning the recommendation is worth an enormous amount to anyone with something to sell, and honestly, I don't think we'd notice if somebody had their thumb on the scales. There's a [controlled study](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adw5578) where people writing alongside a biased AI shifted their own opinions towards the bias — and they still did it after they'd been told the bias was there.

We spent twenty years learning not to trust search results. We skip the sponsored ones. We know the recommended product is the one that paid. None of that instinct has come with us, because this doesn't show up looking like an advert. It shows up looking like the answer.

I'm not going to use it less. But there's one question I've started asking myself, and I'd ask you too: when did you last disagree with it? Not corrected a fact it got wrong — actually pushed back, turned down what it gave you, gone a different way instead. I quite often can't remember. That's the bit that bothers me.
