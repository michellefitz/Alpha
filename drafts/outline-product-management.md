# Post 1 — Product management: outline

Working titles: *Faster Than the Meeting* · *The Constraint Was Never Engineering* · *We Got Good at De-risking*

**The one-line argument:** product management became the practice of de-risking
engineering time; engineering time stopped being scarce; almost nothing got
better — which tells you the constraint was never engineering.

There is a full prose draft of this in `faster-than-the-meeting.md` if you want
to raid it for phrasing. Everything below is the skeleton underneath it.

---

## Narrative arc

Five beats:

1. **A small thing shipped absurdly fast** → the hook
2. **So why was it ever slow?** → the reframe that opens the real question
3. **Because PM had become de-risking** → the diagnosis, with you implicated
4. **So what's the job now?** → the forward-looking half
5. **…except none of it has reached me as a customer** → the nag that closes it

The move that makes it work: beat 5 looks like it undercuts beat 4 but actually
proves it. If capacity wasn't the constraint, judgement always was.

---

## 1. The hook

- Sharing an early release, wanted a feedback box — click a button, box appears,
  emails me
- 30 seconds to explain it. Carried on making breakfast. Live a few minutes later
- Messaged a friend to say it was there
- **"That is ridiculous"** — land on that, don't explain it yet

## 2. The reframe

- The interesting question is not why it's fast now. It's **why it was ever slow**
- Building was never the slow part. A feedback form was never a week of engineering
- The slow part was **the deciding**
- Contrast list — the old process for a genuinely small feature:
  - a week of design
  - research alongside it
  - painstaking interactive prototypes
  - users brought in to test them
  - feedback on very fine details
  - tech plans, estimates
  - release lined up, bugs worked through
- Months of collective attention on something modest

## 3. The diagnosis — de-risking

- So much of product management had quietly become **de-risking**
- Go back through that list and ask what each step was *for*
- Some of it was genuinely about building the right thing. A lot of it was
  **insurance against spending expensive engineering time on something that
  wasn't valuable**
- The prototype existed so we wouldn't build the wrong thing. The research
  existed so someone could point at it if the thing didn't work
- **Concede properly:** that's opportunity cost, it's real, it was always real,
  it still is. Caring about it was not a mistake. The *proportion* was
- **The uncomfortable bit:** if that's what the job largely is, that's the skill
  people get good at. We built a profession that rewards de-risking over
  building good products
  - Your line: *that's why we have a lot of pretty mediocre product managers*
  - Not because they're bad at the job — because they're good at the job **as it
    was actually structured**
  - Decision for you: keep that sharp or soften to the incentive rather than the
    people. Sharp is more quotable, soft is harder to dismiss
- **Implicate yourself:** I got good at de-risking. Enough research to be
  defensible. Enough evidence to survive the question. Designs walked through
  enough rounds that nobody could say they weren't consulted
- **Be fair to it:** it bought certainty. By build time we knew what we were
  building, how long, when it shipped. Marketing lined up, dependencies
  sequenced. That was worth something to a lot of people
- It just cost more than it was worth, most of the time, for most things
- **The hinge line:** now I can build the thing faster than I could get everyone
  in a room to decide whether to build it. Not faster than we could *build* it —
  faster than we could hold the **meeting**

## 4. What the job becomes

- Guard against the bad reading: **this doesn't mean build everything**
- Opportunity cost didn't vanish, it moved — off engineering time, onto your
  attention, and everyone else's
- What changed is *when* the discipline happens. De-risking sat in front of the
  build because the build was the expensive irreversible part. Now **building it
  is often the cheapest way to find out**. The prototype costs less than the
  meeting about the prototype
- So the question stops being *can you get this built* and becomes **can you
  build a good product**
- **That's where good PMs separate from average ones**, and it'll be fast
- The ones who'll do well:
  - spot customer problems genuinely valuable to solve
  - see new patterns forming, new ways to solve things that weren't available
    last year
- The old set — execution, leading teams, navigating ambiguity, stakeholder
  management, running research studies, aggregating feedback
  - That was good work. It was what was needed
  - But much of it existed **because de-risking existed**
  - (my framing, take or leave: scaffolding around a building that's already up)
- What PMs now have to do — step back and ask uncomfortable questions:
  - How tuned in am I really to what makes this good?
  - Do I understand the customer — their actual world, not their persona?
  - Where does this fit in it? What job does it do?
  - What do they actually find valuable?
- **Back to basics**, away from the mechanics: frameworks, ceremonies, artefacts,
  *"all the excessive things that made it look like they were doing more work"*
- What's left is caring about the product, the customer, the details. Which was
  always the job

## 5. The nag that ends it

- For all this efficiency, **shockingly little has reached me as a customer**
- Products I use daily aren't better. Not faster, not less buggy, not richer.
  Some have barely moved
- **Keep the honest hedge:** maybe they are and I'm not noticing. Maybe it's
  arriving slowly. I don't know
- The gap between how much is written about build speed and how little has
  changed in what I open every morning. *What are they all doing with it?*
- **The hypothesis:** the efficiency is going into work that doesn't matter much
- Every team has the list — everything that never cleared the prioritisation bar.
  The bug backlog nobody could justify a sprint for, tidy-ups, small requests,
  the long tail
- We spent years saying *if only we had the capacity*
- Now there's capacity, teams are working down the list, and it isn't landing
- (my line, if you want it: the list was never high-value work — it was things
  that lost, repeatedly, on purpose, to better things)
- Does closing all those bugs actually move anything for the business?
- **The landing:** we treated engineering time as the scarce thing and built a
  discipline around protecting it. It stopped being scarce. The products carried
  on the same. Capacity was never what stood between them and being good
