# The Product Overhang

Here's something I can't square. Engineering has never been faster. I build things myself now, in gaps between school runs, that would have been a fortnight's work for a team a few years ago. Everyone I talk to who builds software says some version of the same thing. And yet almost none of it has reached me as a customer.

The products I open every morning are not noticeably better than they were. Not faster, not less buggy, not richer. Some of them have barely moved. Maybe they have and I'm not seeing it — maybe it's arriving slowly and I'll look up in a year and notice. But the gap between how much gets written about the speed of building and how little has changed in the software I actually use is strange, and I keep turning it over.

The thing I find genuinely surprising, a few years on from ChatGPT, is how *slow* it's been in parts. Some tools take off, change fast, shift how people work. Elsewhere you log in and it's identical. You look around and there are whole categories of product, whole companies, where nothing has changed at all. Plenty of places haven't adopted any of it. Huh, okay.

So where did the speed go?

## The easy answer

The first explanation is that it went into work that doesn't matter much.

Every team has the list — everything that never cleared the prioritisation bar. The bug backlog nobody could justify a sprint for. The tidy-ups, the small requests, the long tail. We spent years saying *if only we had the capacity*, and now there is capacity, and I suspect a lot of teams are working straight down that list. Shipping all of it. And it isn't landing, because the list was never a list of valuable work. It was a list of things that lost, repeatedly and on purpose, to better things.

I think that's partly true. But I don't think it's the main thing. The main thing is a loop we were relying on without ever having built it.

## The loop we didn't know we had

Think about how a roadmap used to run. Six months of work, maybe nine, and everyone reasonably confident in it. You'd ship a v1 of the first thing. Then fixes, improvements, a bit of polish. Feedback would start to arrive. You'd move on to the second problem — and while you were in the middle of solving it, the feedback on the first solution would land. Real usage, real complaints, the thing you'd got wrong, the thing people were doing with it that you hadn't expected.

That's the learning loop, and it was load-bearing. By the time you'd finished problem two, you knew a great deal about problem one, and that shaped problem three. You always had signal arriving from the thing before, and it told you what to do next.

Nobody designed this. It was a side effect of building being slow. The pace of execution *forced* a pace at which there was time to learn. You couldn't move faster than the loop because you couldn't build faster than the loop. The constraint and the cadence were the same thing, and we only ever noticed the constraint.

Now teams tear through the whole roadmap. Months of confident plan, done. And then, with real force: *what's next?*

And it's genuinely hard to answer — not because people have run out of ideas, but because **nothing has been in market long enough to tell you anything**. You've spent your entire stock of informed decisions. Everything left on the table has no evidence behind it yet, because the evidence takes as long to arrive as it always did. You built in weeks. Learning still takes months.

The roadmap was never really a list of things to build. It was a schedule for finding things out, and the building was just the mechanism. We kept the mechanism and sped it up, and lost the schedule.

## A tale of two speeds

Which raises the question sitting underneath all of this: is the market moving faster than execution now, or slower?

I genuinely don't know, and I think the honest answer is *both*. It seems like a tale of two speeds.

On one side, some things are being adopted extraordinarily quickly. Genuine behaviour change, big shifts in how people work, tools that go from nothing to everywhere in months.

On the other side — try selling into a company. Not a startup, a company. There's enormous caution, and a fair amount of fear. What will have to change? Who needs to be trained? Which roles shift, and who's going to be unhappy about it? Has security signed off? Has legal? What happens to the data? None of those questions are unreasonable, and every one of them takes time that has nothing to do with how fast you built the thing.

So it is not the case that you build a great product and, boom, that's it. **You will not get usage and feedback as fast as you built it.** The building compressed. The adopting didn't. And usage that actually tells you something — real usage, not a signup count, the kind that shows you what's wrong — arrives on the slow timeline, not the fast one.

## Where the bottleneck went

The constraint used to be *can we build it*. Now it's *can we get enough real usage, and good enough feedback, to know where to go next*.

That's a different problem, and building faster doesn't help with it. It may actively hurt. Ship more and you know less about any of it, because your attention is spread across a dozen things in market and the signal on each is thin. Ten things launched before the first one has told you anything is not progress. It's noise you'll have to wait through.

We talk a lot about a technology overhang — capability sitting there, unused, because nobody's worked out what to do with it yet. What we have now is a product overhang as well. The products exist. They've been built. They're fine, some of them are good. And people aren't ready.

Underneath it is a ceiling nobody costed. Not the rate at which software can be built — that's been more or less removed — but **the rate of change humans will accept**. The rate at which a person will change how they work, a team will change how it operates, a company will change what it trusts. It was always there. It just used to be hidden behind engineering time, which was slower, so you never hit it.

You can compress the building as much as you like. The part where a person changes what they do doesn't compress. And it turns out that rate is lower than anyone had priced in.
