# The Product Overhang

Engineering has never been faster. I build things myself now, in gaps between school runs, that would have taken a team a fortnight a few years ago. Everyone who builds software says some version of the same thing. And almost none of it has reached me as a customer.

The products I open every morning aren't noticeably better. Not faster, not less buggy, not richer. Maybe they are and I'm not seeing it. But a few years on from ChatGPT, the surprising thing is how slow it's been in parts. Some tools take off. Elsewhere you log in and it's identical. Whole companies where nothing has changed. Huh, okay.

So where did the speed go?

## The easy answer

Into work that doesn't matter much. Every team has the list of things that never cleared the prioritisation bar — the bug backlog, the tidy-ups, the long tail of small requests. We spent years saying *if only we had the capacity*. Now there is, and teams are working down the list. It isn't landing, because the list was never valuable work. It was work that lost, repeatedly, to better things.

That's partly true. It isn't the main thing.

## The loop we didn't know we had

A roadmap used to run something like this. Six or nine months of work. You'd ship a v1 of the first thing, then fixes and improvements. You'd move on to the second problem, and while you were in the middle of it, the feedback on the first would land. Real usage, real complaints, what people were doing with it that you hadn't expected. By the time you finished problem two, you knew a lot about problem one, and that shaped problem three.

Nobody designed that. It was a side effect of building being slow. The pace of execution forced a pace at which there was time to learn. We only ever noticed the constraint, not the cadence.

Now teams tear through the whole roadmap. Then: what's next?

It's hard to answer, and not because anyone has run out of ideas. Nothing has been in market long enough to tell you anything. You built in weeks. Learning still takes months.

## A tale of two speeds

So is the market moving faster than execution now, or slower? I think both.

Some things get adopted extraordinarily fast. Real behaviour change, tools that go from nothing to everywhere in months.

Then try selling into a company. What has to change? Who needs training? Which roles shift? Has security signed off? Has legal? None of those questions are unreasonable, and every one takes time that has nothing to do with how fast you built the thing.

You will not get usage and feedback as fast as you built it. The building compressed. The adopting didn't.

## The long tail leaves

There's a third speed, and it runs the other way.

Most products have a small number of heavy, invested users and a long tail of casual ones — people who use it a little, shallowly, now and then. The shape varies, but the long tail is usually most of the user base.

Those people don't need your product to get better. They need it a bit, occasionally, and now they have somewhere else to go.

I was looking up something for the garden last week. There are dozens of gardening apps. In the past I'd have tried one. Instead I asked Claude — it gave me some layout ideas and a list of plants that would work, and off I went. I was never going to be a paying customer of a gardening app. But I was in their long tail, and I've just left it.

Nobody is going to get Claude or ChatGPT to build them a CRM or a payment system. But the small thing they used to pay for — time tracking, a planner, the point solution — they might build it themselves, or just ask for the answer. AI assistants are going to eat that long tail. For a lot of products that's most of the users, and they'll go quietly.

## The overhang

So the deep end of the market moves slowly, and the shallow end leaves.

We talk about a technology overhang: capability sitting unused because nobody has worked out what to do with it. There's a product overhang now too. The products exist. People aren't ready.

Under it is a limit that was always there, hidden behind engineering time: the rate of change humans will accept. How fast a person will change how they work, a team how it operates, a company what it trusts.

You can compress the building as much as you like. That part doesn't compress. And it turns out it's lower than anyone priced in.
