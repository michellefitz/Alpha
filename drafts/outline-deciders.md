# Post 3 — Directing is the hard part: outline

Working titles: *More Doers Than Deciders* · *Careful What You Wish For* ·
*What's Taking Them So Long?*

**The one-line argument:** "in the AI era you direct rather than execute" is
being sold as a promotion. Directing is the exhausting half. Most organisations
are built almost entirely of doers, and we're about to ask all of them to decide.

---

## Why this is its own post

Post 1 diagnoses what product management *was for* (de-risking engineering time)
and ends on the observation that the efficiency hasn't reached customers.

This post is about **what it's like to live in the new version** — which is a
different subject with a different emotional arc, and it deserves the room.

Two endings that both deflate would fight each other in one post. Kept apart,
post 1 lands an argument and this one lands a warning.

---

## Narrative arc

Six beats, and the shape is a **reversal**:

1. The phrase everyone's repeating — sounds like a promotion
2. What deciding actually looks like from the executing side
3. The reversal: OK, so what are you going to tell them to do?
4. The volume problem: faster teams need *more* deciders, not fewer
5. Who this breaks, and why it goes far beyond product
6. The failure part: cheap execution only works if you can stomach failing a lot

---

## 1. The phrase

- Heard it again on a podcast this week: **in the AI era it's more about
  directing than executing**
- It's everywhere, and it's said as though it's obviously good news
- The unspoken promise: you get to be the one who says what happens
- Set the trap here — don't spring it yet

## 2. What deciding actually looks like

- A PM's job is very often just **making decisions, constantly**
- The team around them executes. They have opinions on the decisions, they want
  to be *part* of the decision — but they don't have to **make** it
- That distinction is the whole post
- **The view from the executing side** — worth writing as the honest, slightly
  unfair thing people actually think:
  - *"What's taking them so long?"*
  - *"Why can't they just make a decision?"*
  - *"Why don't they know?"*
- And then the truth of it: it's genuinely hard. They're balancing a lot of
  things at once, pulling together weak signals and rough estimates, making
  trade-offs, trying to price opportunity cost
- **Knowing what to do is really hard. And making a lot of decisions is tiring**
  — two separate claims, both true, and the second gets almost no attention

## 3. The reversal

- So: people hear "you'll direct rather than execute" and think *great, finally
  I get to tell them what to do*
- **"Okay, cool. What are you going to tell them to do?"**
- And the answer, for a lot of people, is going to be: oh. Actually I don't love
  making all the decisions. Actually some of these are really hard
- The thing nobody says: **a blend of deciding and executing was nicer**
- Executing is where you recover. It's the part with a known answer
- Directing nonstop, with no execution to hide in, is exhausting *and* hard
- And sometimes you just run out — **"I don't know what to do next"**

## 4. The volume problem

- The other thing being said: teams move so much faster now, so do we need fewer
  PMs? Is the role going away?
- Argument runs the other way — **they need more**. More gets done, so more
  decisions are needed to feed it
- "We need the decision makers." Doesn't have to be product people. Just deciders
- What that feels like: people coming to you nonstop. *Make a decision. What
  should we build?*
- You come up with something → it's done in a day, or that week → **"next, next,
  next"**
- **Exciting, then exhausting** — name both, in that order, because the sequence
  is the point
- And the failure mode: if you don't know what to do, **the train grinds to a
  halt**. That's frightening, and it's visible
- So people will make bad decisions under time pressure, because a bad decision
  keeps the train moving and "I don't know yet" doesn't
- Land your line here: **careful what you wish for**

## 5. Who this breaks

- Middle-of-the-road PMs will struggle most, and honestly — a lot of them are
  **executors**
- They run the playbooks of product. Lots of work, much of it repetitive,
  repeating the same playbook
- That's the part that's going away
- **There will be new playbooks** — new ways to approach and think about things —
  but the current ones won't survive
- The distinction worth drawing sharply:
  - **the fundamentals of product become more important than ever**
  - the frameworks, playbooks and operating rituals largely don't
- The old rhythm: a project runs for weeks, and you make decisions at a few small
  points along the way. That rhythm is gone
- **Now widen it past product** — this is the biggest idea in the post:
  - it lands on so many roles, not just PM
  - **there are more doers than deciders, and now everyone has to be a decider**
  - most people just want to get on with their work and get things done
  - they don't want to be asked: *why did you decide to do that work? why that
    way? what was your reasoning? that's not a good idea. your decision failed*
- That's a fundamentally different exposure than most people signed up for

## 6. The failure part

- The optimistic version, and it's real: **when execution is cheap you can try a
  lot of things**, and you can afford to be more tolerant of failure
- But that only works if people can live inside it
- Nobody is used to this: not to doing that many things, not to making that many
  decisions, and not to **failing that many times in public**
- The honest ending: this will feel uncomfortable, nervous-making, threatening,
  frightening — and not because those people are inadequate
- Deciding is hard. We're about to ask far more people to do far more of it,
  faster, with less cover than they've ever had

**Ending options:**
- Land on the reversal: the thing being promised as a promotion is the thing many
  people would least choose
- Or land on the empathy: we built organisations full of people who are good at
  doing the work, and we're about to find out how many of them wanted to decide it
- Or leave it genuinely open, as you did out loud: it's going to be interesting,
  and a lot of people are going to find it really hard

where it has room to be about people rather than about the profession.

Suggested boundary:

  claim that good PMs will separate sharply from average ones — as the *payoff of
  the de-risking argument*
- **Post 3 takes:** playbooks and repetitive execution, who struggles and why,
  the volume of decisions, exhaustion, failure tolerance
