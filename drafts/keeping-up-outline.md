# Outline: How the heck do you keep up?

Working title options:

- You Can't Win That Game

## What changed with this pass

The post now has an ending, and the ending changes what the post *is*. It was "here's a problem and some tests." It's now: **you're playing a game you can't win, almost nobody is winning it, and there's a better game.** That's a far stronger piece, and it's the one people will send to each other.

Consequence: the complaint material — skills pruning, the bitter lesson, the treadmill — demotes from thesis to **evidence**. It's how you earn the ending, not the point. Cut it hard.

## The spine

**1. The fear is real and it's measured.** "Am I falling behind?" isn't neurosis; it's the defining condition of the industry right now, and there's survey data on it.

**2. But it's unwinnable by design.** As soon as you're current, something lands sideways. Chasing releases *feels* like keeping up and isn't.

**3. So change what you're tracking: horizon, not waves.** Direction and pattern over releases and detail. You don't need to be cutting edge unless you work in a lab.

**4. And the way you stay in it is joy, not discipline.** Which is not soft advice — see the survey below. It's the highest-leverage variable anyone has measured.

Keep the shape that worked in *Think With Me Not For Me*: name the trap → mechanism → something portable.

## Narrative arc

### 1. Open on the feeling, precisely

Not "AI is moving fast." Open on the specific, recognisable moment:

> A model drops and within hours there are people on X posting detailed reviews of how it's different, what it's better at, how you should change the way you work with it. And the first thought isn't *interesting* — it's **how the hell have all these people already done this?**

Your line, which is the emotional core:

> "As soon as you stand up and you're up to date on one thing — boom, something else comes across, or a new model, or something comes sideways."

Then the honest admission that buys you the reader: you went looking for a way to keep up, and what you found was that the premise was wrong.

### 2. The pile, and why it stopped working *(compress hard — two short paragraphs)*

This is setup now, not the argument.

- The year everything got good: prototypes → whole products, built by agents. Each jump spawned more to learn — products, models, design tools, skills you build, skills you borrow.
- Learning felt cumulative. Then the models got better and ate it: plan mode, thin skills, hand-built agents.

> "I overdid it with skills — like when you got an iPhone at the start and downloaded every app you saw, then forgot about most of them."

- One line of mechanism, no more: this is Sutton's bitter lesson arriving at your desk. General capability beats hand-crafted scaffolding, reliably, and it always has.
- Two facts that reframe it as normal rather than personal — **Manus has been re-architected five times since March 2024**, and Anthropic strips capability out of Claude Code's own harness as models improve. Everyone rebuilds. It's the job, not a failure.

### 3. Why effort doesn't rescue you

Three reasons, tightly. This is the section that earns the turn.

**Nobody shows you the examples.**

> "So much of their output just talks about the abstract concepts without giving you practical examples. It's hard to get practical examples because they could be used so flexibly."

- Advice arrives as vocabulary — loops, evals, effort levels, context engineering — and almost never as *here is the actual thing, on a real task, start to finish.*
- The reason is real, which is what makes this an argument rather than a gripe: these tools are so flexible that any single example looks arbitrary. **Generality is what makes them powerful and what makes them impossible to teach.**
- So the burden lands on you: derive the practice from the concept, every time, and never know if you derived it right.

**You can't tell what's hard.**

> "I've never handwritten code. I don't know when a coding task is complex or not. Some things that look simple can actually be complex coding, and vice versa."

- Effort dials, model choice and cost control all assume you can estimate difficulty. That estimate comes from doing the work — the part you handed over.
- Note the shape precisely: appearance and difficulty are **decoupled in both directions.** It isn't that everything looks hard. (Dianne Penn calls this the *jagged edge* — worth borrowing the term.)
- Same family as the cognitive debt point from your last post, one level up: not "can you explain the answer" but "can you judge the question."

**And there's no error message for being out of date.**

- The old workflow still runs. Nothing breaks. It's just no longer the best available, and nothing in your day tells you.
- One line back to the last post: same failure mode. The signal is missing, not the capability.
- The kicker: METR's task-horizon paper — the single most-cited source on how fast AI is moving — **went out of date, and its authors had to staple a warning to the front of it.** Even the people measuring the change can't keep their own writing current.

### 4. The turn: nobody is winning this

**The pivot, and the most reassuring section in the post.** Lead with the data — it converts your private worry into a documented condition.

From Lenny Rachitsky and Noam Segal's 2026 Tech Worker Sentiment Survey (thousands of respondents across product, engineering, design, research, data, marketing, sales):

- Asked to describe the state of tech in a sentence, the most common theme was **"chaotic"** — and the sentiment split almost evenly between **excitement and dread.** Same facts, two readings. *That split is your whole post.*
- **77% picked at least one positive and one negative emotion about AI.** The average respondent picked more than five. Their conclusion: *"The defining feeling about AI is ambivalence."*
- The fear isn't what people assume. Only **22%** worry about losing their job to AI. Far more worry about being expected to do more for the same pay (**51%**), getting trapped in an unsustainable pace (**46%**), and the quality of their work dropping (**41%**).
- Burnout jumped from **44.7% to 55.7%** in a single year.
- Their line, which you should quote: *"Everyone agrees the ground is moving. No one is sure yet if it's an earthquake or a launch."*

Then land it:

> The people posting expert takes hours after launch aren't ahead of you. Almost nobody is on top of this. The feeling of being behind is the industry's default state, not a verdict on you.

And name the real trap: **tracking every release feels like keeping up, and isn't.** It's the performance of currency, not the substance of it.

### 5. Look to the horizon

**Your metaphor. It's the best image you have — give it room and don't over-explain it.**

> You're at sea, the boat's bobbing, waves keep hitting you. You can watch every wave, or you can keep your eye on the horizon and your hand on where you're going.

- Don't track releases. Track **direction**: what are these things consistently getting better at, and what does that mean for your product, your work, your opportunities?
- Practical version, and it's a genuinely good method: **look back over the last twelve months, not forward at the last twelve hours.** What actually stuck? A lot lands loud and dampens within a month or two. Waiting is a filter, and it's free.
- Give the reader explicit permission:

> "You don't need to stay on top of every single little detail all the time unless you're actually working in a lab building AI models — and most people aren't. You do not need to be that cutting edge."

- The distinction that makes this coherent rather than lazy: **be strategic about direction, relaxed about detail.** You still owe your setup a periodic re-test — that's where the real cost sits, not in missing a launch.

### 6. Find the joy

The resolution, and the thing you actually want to say.

- Dianne Penn — Anthropic's first technical PM, now Head of Product for their AI Research and Labs teams — devotes a chunk of her recent Lenny's episode to exactly this: *how to find joy in AI when everything is moving this fast.* Someone whose job is literally shipping frontier models thinks the emotional posture is the thing worth talking about.
- **And the survey says that's not soft advice.** Whether people feel *amplified* by AI (49%) or *destabilized/diminished* (19%) predicted how they felt about their career **more than role, seniority, company size, or anything else measured.** Which side of that line you're on is the highest-leverage variable anyone has data on. Joy isn't a nice-to-have; it's the variable.
- Then your practice — and this is the strongest, most stealable thing in the whole post:

> "I keep a standard PRD. When a new model comes out I pull it out and throw it at the new one, just to see what it comes up with. It's a low-effort way to play with them."

- **Say this loudly.** It's a personal benchmark. It's the cheapest possible way to feel a model's difference instead of reading someone else's opinion about it. It costs one prompt. And it answers your own section-3 complaint — you wanted worked examples, and you have one.
- The rest of the posture:
  - Build something for a hobby — a sport, a collection, whatever. Low stakes, no audience.
  - Solve your own problems. Nobody else has to like it.
  - Goof around. Ask ridiculous things, build stupid features, add delights.

> "It's not for anyone else but me, so I can goof around with the models, ask ridiculous things, build stupid features."

- Why this works, and worth stating plainly so it doesn't read as whimsy: a low-stakes side project is the **only** place you'll actually experiment. On real work you use what you know works, because there's a deadline. Play is where calibration comes from — which is the thing you said you were missing.

### 7. Close

Don't tie a bow. Your last post closed on a question and was stronger for it.

Candidate:

> There will always be something new, and you will always be slightly behind. That's the permanent condition, not a phase to get through. The choice isn't whether to keep up — you can't. It's whether the ground moving under you reads as an earthquake or a launch.

The survey's own framing hands you that ending. Use it.

## Open questions for you

**1. Is this one post or two?** You now have enough for two: *the capability overhang and why the guidance fails you* (analytical, critical) and *stop chasing waves, find the joy* (personal, generous). They pull in different emotional directions. My call: **one post, joy as the destination, and cut the analysis to what's needed to earn it** — which is roughly what this outline does. But if the missing-examples argument is the one you care most about, it deserves its own piece and this one gets shorter and better.

**2. Does the PRD ritual become its own thing?** It's your best concrete artifact. You could publish the actual PRD, and what four or five models each did with it. That's the worked example you say nobody provides — and it'd be the most linked thing you've written.

**3. Are you going to make the criticism of the labs?** "They publish concepts, not examples" is mildly confrontational and it's your most original claim. Land it as an observation about the difficulty of teaching flexible tools, not a complaint, and it's strong. Soften it further and it disappears.

**4. What does the horizon actually look like to you?** The metaphor promises a payoff the outline can't supply — you have to name the trend *you* see and what it means for your space. One concrete call. Without it the section is a nice image and no content.
