# Outline: How the heck do you keep up?

Structure, points to hit, and supporting material. No drafted prose — all wording is yours.

Working titles:
- **Keep Your Eye on the Horizon**
- The Overhang Is Behind You
- You Can't Win That Game
- Don't Obsess

---

## The argument in five moves

1. Trying to keep up with everything doesn't just fail — it **burns you out**. There's an arc, and it ends in fatigue and cynicism.
2. It's unwinnable by design, and **nobody is winning it.** There's survey data.
3. It's also **aimed at the wrong thing.** The unexplored capability is in what shipped three months ago, not in what shipped today.
4. So **scope your attention.** Pick a few areas. Drop the rest deliberately, not guiltily.
5. And **sustain it through play**, because discipline won't hold and joy will.

## Narrative arc

### 1. Open — the recognisable moment

**Does:** puts the reader inside the feeling before you name it.

Points to hit:
- A model drops; within hours there are detailed reviews, comparisons, revised advice on how to work with it.
- The first reaction isn't curiosity, it's *how have all these people already done this?*
- The treadmill never lets you stand up — you get current on one thing and something lands sideways.
- Signal early that this isn't another "here's how to keep up" post; you went looking for that and concluded the premise was wrong.

Your raw material:
> "As soon as you stand up and you're up to date on one thing — boom, something else comes across, or a new model, or something comes sideways."

### 2. The fatigue arc

**Does:** the first genuinely new thing you're saying. Most posts stop at "it's hard"; you're describing what happens to a person who tries anyway.

Points to hit:
- The stages, in order: energised → deep in it → "I've got this" → slowing down → feeling behind again → fatigue → jaded → losing the interest that got you in.
- The cruel bit: **feeling on top of it is what precedes falling behind.** The moment of mastery is the setup.
- Losing the excitement is the real cost. Worse than being out of date, because the excitement is what powered the learning.
- You cannot go deep on everything — models, capabilities, ways of working, tools — and it isn't a discipline problem. It's arithmetic.

Substance available:
- **"Change fatigue"** is an established concept in organisational psychology and it maps onto your arc almost exactly: exhaustion, cynicism and disengagement from sustained change without recovery time. Prosci's framing — *"at least one change project past their capacity, which can result in burnout, indifference, retreat, or looking for a way out"* — is your four end-states. Fair warning on sourcing: it's a legitimate concept ([Wikipedia has an entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_fatigue)) but the top results are change-management consultancies, not papers. Name the concept, don't lean on their stats.
- **Hard numbers from the 2026 Tech Worker Sentiment Survey** (see sources): significant burnout rose **44.7% → 55.7% in one year**, and **46%** name being trapped in an unsustainable pace as a top fear — ahead of losing their job to AI (22%). Your fatigue prediction is already measurable in the population.

### 3. Nobody is winning this

**Does:** the pivot. Converts private worry into a documented condition. **Reach this by roughly the halfway mark** or readers bail during the complaint.

Points to hit:
- The people posting expert takes hours after launch aren't ahead of you.
- Feeling behind is the industry's default state, not a verdict on you.
- Name the real trap: **tracking every release feels like keeping up and isn't.** It's the performance of currency, not the substance.

Substance available (all from the survey, all quotable):
- Most common one-word description of the state of tech: **"chaotic"** — sentiment split almost evenly between **excitement and dread**. Same facts, two readings. That split is the hinge of your whole post.
- **77%** picked at least one positive *and* one negative emotion; the average respondent picked more than five. Their conclusion: *"the defining feeling about AI is ambivalence."*
- The fears are about pace and quality, not replacement: more work for same pay **51%**, unsustainable pace **46%**, quality dropping **41%**, job loss to AI only **22%**.
- Their framing line: *"Everyone agrees the ground is moving. No one is sure yet if it's an earthquake or a launch."*

### 4. The overhang is behind you

**Does:** your strongest new idea, and the one that reframes the reader's whole strategy. Give it room.

Points to hit:
- There is enormous unexplored capability in models that shipped months ago. Most people never got near the edge of what they already have.
- Chasing the new release skips over capability you already own and haven't touched.
- So the honest question isn't "what's new?" — it's **"what can the thing on my desk already do that I've never tried?"**
- This also defuses the FOMO: you're not missing the frontier by ignoring a launch. The frontier you haven't explored is the one already in your hands.

Substance available — and this grounds your instinct in a real, credible term:
- **"Capability overhang" is Jack Clark's term** (Anthropic co-founder, author of the *Import AI* newsletter), and it means precisely what you're reaching for: at release, **not even the developers know the full extent of a model's capability surface** — it gets discovered collectively, by users, over time. [Simon Willison's quote page](https://simonwillison.net/2023/Mar/22/jack-clark/) has the original; Clark has returned to it repeatedly in [Import AI](https://jack-clark.net/).
- The strong version of the point: chain-of-thought prompting was *discovered by users*, in models that already shipped. The capability was there the whole time. That's the overhang made concrete, and it's the best possible illustration of "the untapped thing is behind you."
- Using Clark's term does double duty — it names your idea and it borrows an Anthropic co-founder's authority for a claim readers might otherwise treat as self-soothing.

### 5. Scope your attention deliberately

**Does:** turns the relief into a decision the reader can actually make. This is the practical heart.

Points to hit:
- You can't follow everything, so choose — explicitly, not by drift.
- The areas worth naming:
  - **the product you work on**
  - **how you work** — ways of working, using AI to make your own work more efficient
  - **a personal project**
- Models themselves: **no**, unless they're directly relevant to your work. Say this plainly; it's the permission most readers need and won't give themselves.
- The point about deliberateness: dropping something on purpose is a decision. Dropping it by exhaustion is a failure. Same outcome, completely different relationship to it.
- Worth pre-empting: this isn't "stop learning." It's spending the same attention on fewer things and actually getting to depth.

### 6. Horizon, not waves

**Does:** the image the post is named for. Don't over-explain it — state it and move to the method.

Points to hit:
- The boat at sea: waves hitting constantly; watch every wave or keep your eye on the horizon and your hand on where you're going.
- Track **direction and pattern**, not releases: what are these things consistently getting better at?
- Then the translation, which is the part that matters: what does that direction mean for **your** product, your work, the opportunities you're seeing?
- The method, and it's genuinely good: **look back over the last twelve months, not forward at the last twelve hours.** What actually stuck? Lots lands loud and dampens within a month or two. Waiting is a free filter.
- Explicit permission: you don't need to be cutting edge unless you work in a lab building models, and most people don't.
- The distinction that stops this reading as laziness: **strategic about direction, relaxed about detail.**

**Your two horizon calls.** This is the payoff the metaphor promises. Two is the right number — enough to be a real claim, few enough to stay a blog post and not a forecast.

**Call one: intelligence keeps getting cheaper, unevenly.**

Points to hit:
- You won't always have the bleeding-edge model, and that's fine. The thing that matters is that **mass-available intelligence keeps getting better while staying cheap.**
- There will be crunch points — the fall isn't smooth.
- Implication for the reader: don't architect around today's cost. The thing that's too expensive to do at scale this year probably isn't next year.

Substance — and this is a strong, credible primary source:
- **[Epoch AI, "LLM inference prices have fallen rapidly but unequally across tasks"](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends)** (Cottier, Snodin, Owen, Adamczewski, March 2025).
  - The price to reach **GPT-4's performance on PhD-level science questions fell 40x per year.**
  - Across six benchmarks the decline ranges from **9x to 900x per year**, depending on the capability milestone.
  - The concrete ladder in their data: GPT-3-level performance on MMLU cost **$60 per million tokens in November 2021** and **$0.07 by October 2024**. Same capability, roughly 850x cheaper in under three years.
  - **Their title is your nuance** — "unequally across tasks." That's your crunch points, in their data.
  - Epoch's own caution, which you should carry: the very fastest drops happened in the most recent year, "so it's less clear that those will persist."
- ⚠️ The page is from March 2025 and it's now September 2026 — check whether they've refreshed before quoting a figure. "LLMflation" (a16z's term, ~10x/year at a fixed quality bar) is a usable shorthand but a weaker citation than Epoch.

**The counter you should handle** — it makes the call sharper, not weaker:
- Unit price collapses while **total spend rises**. Reasoning models burn far more tokens per task, so cheaper intelligence gets consumed faster than it gets cheap. Classic Jevons paradox.
- So "intelligence is getting cheap" and "my bill keeps going up" are both true, and the reader feels the second one. Naming that tension is more honest than the clean version — and it links directly to the cost frustration in the Choices Without Guidance post (`outline-choices-without-guidance.md`).

**Call two: one-to-one becomes many-to-many.**

Points to hit:
- Today it's mostly solo: you and your agent. That's the anomaly, not the destination.
- Where it goes: every product has humans *and* agents using it. Agents talking to agents. Two people and an agent on one piece of work. You working with several agents that have different skills.
- The shift to name: **from one-to-one to many-to-many.** People, agents, all interacting — and the experience stops being solitary.
- Implication for the reader: if you build products, the second user is already arriving and it isn't a person.

Substance — **your prediction is less speculative than you think; the plumbing is being laid now:**
- **[Linux Foundation, 9 April 2026](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/a2a-protocol-surpasses-150-organizations-lands-in-major-cloud-platforms-and-sees-enterprise-production-use-in-first-year)** — the A2A (Agent-to-Agent) protocol at its one-year mark:
  - **150+ organizations** supporting it; v1.0 stable spec shipped.
  - Embedded by all three major clouds — Microsoft (Azure AI Foundry, Copilot Studio) and AWS (Bedrock AgentCore Runtime), alongside Google.
  - Production deployments in supply chain, financial services, insurance and IT operations.
  - Agents built on different platforms (LangGraph, CrewAI) can now "work together, delegate sub-tasks, and coordinate complex workflows **without sharing internal memory**."
  - **The line that is your argument, from their release:** *"As software systems operate more independently, coordination becomes the bottleneck."*
- **Agents are getting identity and a wallet**, which is the real tell:
  - **Signed Agent Cards** for cryptographic identity verification — agents need verifiable identity, which only matters if they're dealing with strangers.
  - **Agent Payments Protocol (AP2)** for agent-driven transactions, already backed by **60+ organizations** across payments and financial services.
- Complementary standard: MCP handles agent-to-tool (vertical), A2A handles agent-to-agent (horizontal). The pairing is deliberate. *(A widely repeated figure puts MCP at ~97M monthly SDK downloads by March 2026 — striking, but I only found it in secondary sources. Verify before using.)*

**The counter you should handle:**
- Multi-agent systems have over-promised before, and coordination overhead is real — more agents often means more failure modes, not more capability.
- But note where the bottleneck actually moves: not capability, **trust**. Identity, permissions, liability, who pays when an agent gets it wrong. Signed Agent Cards and AP2 exist precisely because the industry already knows this.
- That's a more interesting claim than "there will be lots of agents," and it's defensible.

### 7. Why you can't tell what's hard

**Does:** your most personal material. Keep it, but it's now in service of the argument rather than the argument itself — it's *why* scoping and playing matter.

Points to hit:
- You've never handwritten code, so you can't judge whether a task is complex.
- Precise shape: appearance and difficulty are **decoupled in both directions**. Simple-looking things that are hard, hard-looking things that are trivial.
- Consequence: effort levels, model choice and cost control all assume an estimate you can't make.
- The reframe — and this is the bit worth landing: **this isn't a non-coder problem.**

Substance available — a proper peer-reviewed field experiment, and it's excellent for you:
- **Dell'Acqua et al., "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier"** (Harvard Business School / BCG, published in *Organization Science*). **758 highly skilled management consultants**, preregistered randomised experiment.
  - On 18 tasks **inside** the AI's frontier: **12.2% more tasks completed, 25.1% faster**, significantly better quality.
  - On a task **outside** it: consultants using AI were **19% *less* likely to reach the correct answer** than those without.
  - The finding that matters to you: the frontier is **jagged and invisible**. Experienced professionals, in their own domain, could not tell which side of it a task fell on — and over-relied on the output as a result.
- So: highly experienced consultants couldn't see the frontier in their own field. Your inability to judge coding difficulty isn't because you don't code. **Nobody can see it.** That's a much more generous — and more accurate — framing than the one you've been carrying.
- Dianne Penn uses **"the jagged edge"** in her episode title; Mollick's group coined "jagged frontier" in this paper. Worth using the term and citing the study.
- Related idea: cognitive debt — not "can you explain the answer" but "can you judge the question." The MIT study behind it ([Your Brain on ChatGPT](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/)) was only in the archived agreement-loop post, so cite it directly here if you want it rather than as a callback.

Papers: [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4573321) · [free PDF via MIT Sloan](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/SSRN-id4573321.pdf) · [Organization Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838)

### 8. Find the joy

**Does:** the resolution. The thing you actually want to say.

Points to hit:
- Fun over pressure. If the pressure is on the work side, put the fun in personal projects — that's where you have control, freedom and permission to do mad things.
- Your concrete list (keep it specific, it's what makes this land):
  - image collages and collections
  - videos made from family photos, for people in your family to enjoy
  - games with your kids built from their ridiculous ideas
  - a product for a hobby you actually have
- Your PRD ritual — **the most stealable thing in the post.** A standing PRD you throw at each new model to see what it makes. One prompt, personal benchmark, and you *feel* the difference instead of reading someone's opinion of it.
- No audience, no stakes. Ridiculous requests, stupid features, delights.
- The mechanism, so it doesn't read as whimsy: **low stakes is the only place you'll actually experiment.** On real work you use what you know works, because there's a deadline. Play is where calibration comes from — the exact thing section 7 says you're missing.
- The bleed-over: little by little it carries into work. Frame as a side effect, not the justification — the moment play has to justify itself in work terms, it stops working.

Substance available — and this is what stops "find the joy" being dismissed as soft:
- **The survey's headline finding.** Whether people felt **amplified** by AI (49.0%) or **destabilized/diminished** (13.9% + 5.0%) predicted how they felt about their career **more than role, seniority, company size, or anything else measured.** Which side of that line you land on is the highest-leverage variable in the dataset. Lead with this and joy becomes a strategic claim, not a nice one.
- **Dianne Penn** — Anthropic's first technical PM, now Head of Product for their AI Research and Labs teams — gives this real time in her Lenny's episode; *"How to find joy in AI when everything is moving this fast"* is a listed chapter. Someone whose job is shipping frontier models thinks the emotional posture is the thing worth discussing. Pull the exact quote yourself — you heard it, and the transcript is JS-gated so I couldn't.

### 9. Close

Points to land:
- You will always be slightly behind. That's the permanent condition, not a phase.
- The choice isn't whether to keep up. It's what you point your attention at, and whether the moving ground reads as threat or as interesting.
- Don't tie a bow — *When Did You Last Disagree With AI?* closes on a question and is stronger for it.
